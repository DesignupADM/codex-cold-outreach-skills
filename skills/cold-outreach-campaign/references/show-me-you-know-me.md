# Show Me You Know Me®

Use this reference when writing high-value outreach to executives or strategic buyers who require more than generic personalization.

## Core principle

Show Me You Know Me® is the practice of understanding the buyer as:

1. a human being
2. a company stakeholder
3. a participant in their market or industry

This is not about collecting random facts. It is about building messages that feel informed, relevant, and grounded in the real context of the buyer's role and environment.

## Why it matters

The average executive inbox is flooded with generic outreach. Many emails are built around vague subject lines, boilerplate intros, and miniature versions of personalization that could be sent to hundreds of people.

The difference between a normal cold email and a strong Show Me You Know Me® email is that the recipient understands instantly that you did your homework and that the outreach is relevant to them.

## Benchmark context from the field

The source material reports:

- executive email open rates average around 6%
- executive reply rates average around 0.9%
- Show Me You Know Me® style emails often reach average open rates around 43%
- comparable reply rates can approach 20%

These numbers are not universal guarantees, but they illustrate the competitive advantage of research-based outreach when it is done well.

## The three research layers

### 1. The human

Research the buyer as a person before writing the email.

Good sources:

- LinkedIn profile and headline
- About section and work history
- common connections or shared context
- personal interests, posts, or professional themes
- CRM history and prior engagement with your company

Look for authentic common ground, such as:

- a relevant role transition
- a shared background or connection
- a professional topic they openly discuss
- a personal or professional interest that connects naturally to your offer

The goal is to find a real human tie, not a forced one.

### 2. The company

After understanding the person, learn what matters at the company level.

Good sources:

- website and key pages
- press releases, blogs, and recent announcements
- product or service positioning
- customer evidence or public case studies
- executive commentary, events, and interviews
- prior customer relationship history or churn/renewal context

Useful questions:

- What does this company care about right now?
- What challenges or growth motions are visible?
- Has the company recently expanded, hired, launched, shifted strategy, or changed pricing?

### 3. The space

Understand how the buyer thinks and speaks in their market or vertical.

Examples:

- law firms often talk in terms of clients, business development, and counsel rather than leads
- sales and marketing teams may use pipeline language, conversion, retention, or CAC more directly
- vertical-specific terminology matters because it cues credibility and relevance

This is where you adapt your language and framing to fit the industry, not just the company.

## Good examples of personalization

Strong personalization feels like this:

- a specific company or role trigger
- a relevant personal or professional fact tied to the buyer's context
- a mention of an executive's public work, insight, or company motion
- a specific issue or opportunity that connects directly to the offer

Examples:

- `From a friend of Jim Durham`
- `Switzerland + Le Dip Cheeseburger + [company name]`
- `Bill, I saw your recent post about your adorable furry companion Sprinkles...`

The key is not just that the reference is personal. It must also be relevant and supportive of the message.

## Poor personalization to avoid

Avoid anything that feels forced, generic, or self-serving.

Examples of weak personalization:

- `Go Noles!` tied to a college mascot with no business relevance
- `Quick question` or `Want to collaborate?` with no context
- personal details pulled from private or loosely relevant sources
- links to a buyer's personal life that do not relate to the problem you are trying to solve
- generic subject lines that could be sent to 500 people at once

This kind of personalization often does more harm than good because it signals low effort and low understanding.

## Personalization at scale is not the same as real personalization

A subject line like:

- `Quick question`
- `Meeting on Tuesday?`
- `Need more pipeline?`
- `Want to collaborate?`

is not meaningfully personalized. It may look customized but it is not anchored in anything real about the prospect or their context.

The goal is to make the buyer think, "This person actually knows something about my situation," not "This email is just another spray-and-pray note."

## Subject line strategy

The subject line is the first and often most important element of the email because it decides whether the message gets opened.

Use it to signal relevance, not just curiosity.

Strong patterns:

- personalized references that are contextually meaningful
- short, sharp, specific framing
- references that are clear to the buyer and private to the audience segment
- a signpost that the email is not generic

Examples:

- `From a friend of Jim Durham`
- `Switzerland + Le Dip Cheeseburger + [company name]`
- `ΠΚΦ + Nothing Shall Ever Tear Us Asunder (Except Detention Fees)`

Rules:

- shorter is not always better
- `+` separators can help create a pattern-break structure
- specificity and relevance beat generic cleverness

## First sentence guidance

The email's first sentence should complement the subject line and make the preview text feel valuable.

Two effective patterns:

### 1. Introduce context and credibility

Example:

```text
Hi, Bill. We have yet to be properly introduced, but I'm Sam McKenna, and...
```

This works because it signals that the sender knows the buyer and is not just sending a generic note.

### 2. Reference a real signal quickly

Example:

```text
Bill, I saw your recent post about your adorable furry companion Sprinkles, and...
```

This works when the personal reference is authentic and tied to the sender's own context.

## Value proposition guidance

The value proposition is not simply what you do as a company. It is the challenge you solve and the outcome you create for this particular buyer.

Weak value prop:

- `We help companies grow faster.`

Stronger value prop:

- `If you're anything like our clients, you see LinkedIn Sales Navigator usage hover around five percent of your licenses. We can help your team increase adoption and improve conversion efficiency.`

The strongest email does not stop at describing the service. It anticipates the likely objection and addresses it in the same breath.

## Close without being presumptuous

Avoid using a calendar link as the default ask in the first outreach.

Avoid lines such as:

- `Do you have 15 minutes tomorrow?`
- `Can we jump on a call Monday at 1 PM?`

These can feel presumptuous and put the burden back on the buyer.

Better pattern:

```text
Do you have time over the next week or two to learn more? Let me know what works for you, and I'll send a calendar invite accordingly.
```

This respects the buyer's schedule while keeping momentum.

## Sequence strategy and timing

The source material emphasizes quality over quantity, especially for high-value executives.

Recommended pattern:

- research during the week
- send first email on Thursday or Friday for high-value exec outreach
- send the second email within 48 hours
- do not change the subject line for that follow-up
- keep the second email short and concise

The second email should not be a second novel. It is a reminder and a chance to re-signal relevance.

Example follow-up:

```text
Hey Josh. Wanted to quickly pop in and see if you've read my note below. I'd still be grateful for the chance to learn more about how #samsales can support [company name].
```

## LinkedIn in the sequence

The LinkedIn touch should feel contextual and respectful, not spammy.

Avoid:

- profile views as a sequence habit
- blanket InMails that feel sales-y
- pitch-slapping after a connection request is accepted

Better pattern:

```text
My name might look familiar as I've sent you a few emails. I'd still appreciate the chance to learn more about how #samsales can support [organization name]. If you're ever open to a chat, please let me know.
```

This signals context, human effort, and a real reason for the outreach.

## Discovery call opening

Once the buyer replies, the Show Me You Know Me® approach should continue through the conversation.

Example structure:

```text
Thank you so much for making time for us today. I could tell you a million things about #samsales, but I'd love to hear about you first.
```

Then add a few relevant observations:

- `I know you recently had an acquisition.`
- `I know you're looking to double revenue.`
- `I recently saw that you replaced your CRO.`

Then ask for permission to learn more:

```text
Tell me about your team and your challenges. What's the overall landscape like for you, if that's okay?
```

This keeps the call grounded in their reality rather than a feature-and-benefit dump.

## What to do in person

For in-person meetings, preparation and logistics matter.

Best practices:

- make the reservation when possible
- add notes to the reservation with check timing preferences
- arrive early
- position the meeting so you are not awkwardly trapped at the wrong table or with your back to the room

Small touches like this reinforce your professionalism and reduce unnecessary friction.

## Practical takeaway

Use Show Me You Know Me® to do three things well:

- make the buyer feel seen as a person
- prove that you understand the company and problem context
- deliver a message that looks thoughtful enough to earn a reply

When done well, this approach creates stronger relationships, improves response rates, and creates a better long-term sales motion than generic mass personalization.

## Output standard for this skill

When the user asks for a Show Me You Know Me® style email, default to:

- a strong topic-specific subject line
- a relevant first line based on real context
- a short but specific value proposition tied to the buyer's situation
- a respectful CTA that respects the buyer's schedule
- a follow-up pattern that reinforces relevance instead of repeating the first email
